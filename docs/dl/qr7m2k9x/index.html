Lead Routing & SLA Playbook

How to get every inbound lead to the right owner in seconds, put a clock on it, and make the clock enforce itself.

This is a working document for the person who owns the routing rules: a RevOps or sales ops lead, or a founder doing that job part-time. It assumes you have a CRM with workflow automation (HubSpot, Salesforce, or similar) and a Slack or Teams workspace. It does not assume a dedicated routing tool, although section 4 tells you when one starts paying for itself.

Read sections 1 to 3 once. Sections 5 to 8 are the parts you'll come back to. Every deadline, grace period and target in here is an example operating choice to adapt and test against your own arrival patterns and staffing, not an industry standard.

Section 1Why minutes matter

Two studies underpin everything in this playbook, and they are worth knowing precisely because they are usually misquoted.

MIT Sloan / InsideSales.com, 2007. Dr James Oldroyd analysed roughly 15,000 leads and over 100,000 call attempts across six companies. The odds of making contact with a lead were about 100 times higher when the first call went out within 5 minutes than at 30 minutes, and the odds of qualifying the lead were about 21 times higher. Between 5 and 10 minutes the qualification odds had already fallen roughly fourfold.

Harvard Business Review, 2011. Oldroyd and colleagues audited 2,241 US companies by submitting a test lead to each. The average response time among those that responded at all was 42 hours; 23 percent never responded. Firms that contacted the lead within an hour were nearly 7 times more likely to have a meaningful conversation with a decision maker than those that waited even one more hour, and about 60 times more likely than those waiting 24 hours or longer.

Three honest caveats before you build a business case on this:

  • The primary data is old. It predates chat, calendar-booking links and most marketing automation. Vendors of routing and AI-SDR tools republish the numbers every year, sometimes with their own data attached, and their incentives are obvious. Treat the direction as settled and the exact multiples as directional.
  • The studies measured call attempts. If your first touch is an email, the clock still matters, but the mechanism (catching the buyer while they are still at their desk thinking about the problem) is weaker. Where you can call, call.
  • Speed helps qualification and contact rates. It is not evidence that faster response closes more revenue on its own; the buyer still has to be a fit. Speed removes a reason to lose; it does not create a reason to win.

The practical conclusion is unchanged: whatever your current time to first touch is, it is almost certainly measured in hours when it should be measured in minutes, and the first thing to do is measure it.

Section 2The service agreement

A demo request arrives at 16:55 on a Friday. It matches two territories, the account owner is away, and nobody knows whether the clock runs over the weekend. An assignment rule can send that request somewhere; it cannot settle those questions. Settle them first, in writing, on one page. Sales and operations should be able to describe the same answers without opening a workflow.

Which requests count?
Start with explicit commercial requests: demo and contact-sales forms, partner introductions, customer expansion enquiries. Give content downloads, event lists, support tickets and bulk imports their own handling rules. Record why anything is excluded.
Who owns the response?
One accountable person at all times. A pool distributes work; its duty manager remains accountable until a rep takes ownership. Keep "who responds" separate from "who owns the account" where those differ.
What counts as first touch?
A logged call attempt to a usable number, or a rep-sent email addressing the enquiry, or a booked meeting. An internal task, an owner change, an automated receipt, or enrolment in a sequence does not. Track a successful conversation separately.
When does the clock start?
At the earliest trustworthy receipt time of the request, not when it reached the CRM. Keep the CRM arrival time as well, so a slow integration is visible rather than hidden. Enrichment and assignment must never postpone the start.
When does the clock run?
Choose elapsed minutes for a service staffed around the clock, or covered minutes for stated working hours. Define the timezone, weekdays, holidays and backup rota for each calendar. This playbook uses covered minutes throughout.
Who handles a miss?
Name the on-duty manager and an available backup, per calendar. Agree the escalation interval, the reassignment conditions, and who is allowed to pause the automation.
Choose a promise the team can keep. An unmet 5-minute SLA is worse than a met 15-minute one, because reps learn to ignore the alerts. Set the first targets from a measured baseline (section 9, week 1), then tighten.

Section 3Design principles

Route on the fewest fields that predict ownership

Every dimension you add to the matrix doubles the number of rows you have to maintain and halves the chance the matrix survives the next reorg. Four dimensions cover nearly every B2B team: region, company size band, product line, and inbound channel. If you find yourself adding industry, lead score bands and job title, stop and ask whether those belong in prioritisation (who gets called first) rather than routing (who owns it).

Scoring and routing are different jobs

Lead scoring estimates fit and intent. Routing decides who owns the record and which SLA applies. A score can feed a routing decision (for example, enterprise-scored leads skip the SMB pool) but if the two are entangled in one rule set, every scoring tweak silently changes ownership. Keep them in separate workflows with a clear handoff.

Availability is a routing input, not an afterthought

A lead assigned to someone on leave, in a four-hour offsite, or already sitting on 40 open leads is an unassigned lead with extra steps. The router should check working hours, out-of-office status and open-lead count before it assigns. If your CRM can't do that natively, the escalation ladder in section 6 is your safety net, but it is a net, not a design.

Something, not someone, enforces the clock

SLAs that depend on a manager noticing are not SLAs. The timer starts on lead creation, escalation fires automatically, and reassignment happens without a human deciding to do it. Humans set the policy; the system runs it.

Resolve identity before you distribute work

Before any segment rule runs, check the domain against existing accounts, customers and open opportunities. If the match is ambiguous, send the request straight to triage. Normalise country, employee count, product and channel, and use an explicit "unknown" value where data is missing: an unknown company size is not a small company, and a missing region should block only the rules that need a region, never a verified account owner's right to respond.

Every lead matches a row

A catch-all row routing to a staffed triage rep is mandatory, and the triage rep has a deadline too: make the first touch, then resolve ownership. An internal transfer alone does not meet the SLA. Triage volume is a metric; if more than a handful of leads a week land there, the matrix has a gap.

Section 4Distribution models

Within a queue, you still have to pick which rep gets the lead. There are four models in common use and most teams end up with a blend.

ModelHow it assignsBest forWatch out for
Simple round robinNext rep in rotation, every time.Small, homogeneous pools of 3 to 10 reps with similar skill and capacity.Ignores everything about the rep except queue position: assigns to people on leave and to people already overloaded.
Weighted round robinEach rep has a weight; share of leads = weight divided by sum of weights.Ramping new hires (0.5), part-timers, senior reps who carry other duties.Use it for planned, documented differences. Using weights to encode untracked opinions about performance breeds resentment fast.
Capacity-basedSkips reps above an open-lead or open-opportunity cap; resumes when they clear.Teams where deal cycles vary a lot, so "same number of leads" is not "same workload".Needs a clean definition of "open". Stale records inflate capacity and starve good reps.

A sensible default for most B2B teams: named accounts and existing customers first (direct ownership), then weighted, availability-aware round robin inside each segment pool. Move beyond CRM-native routing when any of these is true: your team exceeds roughly 15 to 20 reps, deal sizes vary by an order of magnitude across segments, routing needs enrichment data or calendar checks, or you are maintaining more than about ten exceptions by hand. Dedicated tools (LeanData, Chili Piper, Default and similar) exist for exactly that point; before then they are overhead.

Whatever the model, the assignment must be real-time. A workflow that batches every 15 minutes has already spent your entire SLA before the rep hears about the lead.

Worked example

A new NA prospect with 500 employees submits a Core demo request. No account, customer or partner match, so rule 70 assigns the mid-market pool with a 10-minute clock. The same request with 501 employees is rule 50: enterprise pool, 5 minutes. The same request as a validated partner referral is rule 40, regardless of size: partner desk, 30 minutes. Test the boundary values (50, 51, 500, 501), not just a typical company.

How to adapt it:

  • Keep a calendar and pool register alongside the matrix: for each calendar label, the real timezone, service hours, holidays, eligible reps, duty manager and backup. "NA" and "EMEA" are illustrations, not coverage plans for whole continents.
  • Start from your last 90 days of leads, tagged with the four dimensions. Keep the 6 to 10 rows that cover 90 percent of volume. Add the catch-all. Ship that. Add rows only when the triage queue tells you to.
  • Company size from a form field is unreliable. Where you have enrichment (Clearbit, ZoomInfo, Apollo or similar), route on the enriched value and fall back to the form value.
  • Region should follow the lead's company location, not the submitter's IP. A US buyer travelling in Lisbon is still NA.
  • The CSV has columns for out-of-hours handling and second-stage escalation. Fill them in before you go live; they are where most breaches actually happen.

Section 6The clock and the ladder

Setting the targets

Given the evidence, 5 minutes during working hours is the right target for anything high-value or high-intent, and 15 to 30 minutes is defensible for volume queues where reps are also working a pipeline. Don't set a target you can't staff: an unmet 5-minute SLA is worse than a met 15-minute one, because reps learn to ignore the alerts.

Covered minutes: a worked example

Coverage is Monday to Friday, 09:00 to 17:00 Europe/Dublin. A request arrives Friday at 16:55 with a 15-minute SLA. Five covered minutes pass on Friday; the remaining ten end at 09:10 on Monday. The buyer has waited 64 hours and 15 minutes of elapsed time. An automated receipt sent on Friday does not stop the clock. A rep email at 09:08 Monday meets the promise; at 09:12 it misses by two covered minutes. Report covered and elapsed time side by side, so weekend waiting stays visible and you can decide whether it's acceptable.

Store the absolute deadline the moment the rule is selected, computed from the original receipt time and the saved calendar. If a request reaches the CRM after its deadline has already passed, flag the breach immediately; starting a fresh timer would hide the integration delay. Never reset the deadline when enrichment finishes, a rep goes offline, or ownership changes. If policy allows a revised deadline, keep the original and record who approved the change.

The escalation ladder

Escalation is a sequence with fixed intervals measured in multiples of the SLA, so one rule set works for every tier.

  1. T + 0Lead created, matched, assigned. Rep notified where they actually work (Slack DM or mobile push), with name, company, source, one enrichment fact and the suggested first move. A CRM task alone is not a notification.
  2. T + SLAStill unworked: post to the escalation channel and re-notify the rep. Record the breach on the lead.
  3. T + 2×SLANotify the manager. Reassign to the next available rep in the pool. Log the reassignment with reason.
  4. T + 4×SLARoute to the manager pool. Manager owns the first touch personally. This should be rare; if it isn't, the pool is understaffed for the SLA.

Rules that stop the ladder misbehaving

  • Cooldown. A reassigned lead cannot be reassigned again for at least one full SLA interval. Without this, leads ping-pong.
  • Working-hours awareness. The clock pauses outside the queue's working hours and resumes when the queue opens. A form submitted at 23:00 is not a breach at 23:05; it is a breach at 09:05.
  • Holdouts. Existing customers and open opportunities never enter new-business routing, regardless of form. Check the domain against customers first.
  • One clock per lead. Cancel the timer on first touch, using the section 2 definition. Opening the record, creating a task, or an automated receipt does not count.
  • At most one automated rescue. If the backup is unavailable or a transfer is unsafe (named accounts and open opportunities often need manager approval), create a manager intervention task and keep the current accountable owner visible. Never circulate a request between empty queues.
  • A breach stays a breach. A late first touch is recorded as late. The only correction is trusted evidence that the response actually happened on time and was logged late; keep the correction history and mark the earlier alert superseded.

Logic, in pseudocode

This is the shape of the automation, not executable CRM code. Your platform has to supply durable scheduling, calendar arithmetic, activity matching and atomic state changes; the notes for each platform follow.

on eligible_request_received(event):
    request = create_once(event.source, event.request_id)   # atomic; a replayed event cannot make a second clock
    if request.already_exists: return
    if is_customer(request.domain) or has_open_opportunity(request.domain):
        rule = holdout_rule_for(request)                    # rules 10, 20: direct to the existing owner
    elif ambiguous_account_match(request):
        rule = TRIAGE
    else:
        rule = first_matching_row(matrix, request)          # ordered by priority; TRIAGE if none
    rep = pick(rule.owner_queue, rule.model)                # weighted / availability-aware / direct / backup
    save request.matched_rule, rule.version, rule.calendar, rule.sla_minutes
    request.due_at = add_covered_minutes(request.received_at, rule.sla_minutes, rule.calendar)
    if now > request.due_at: record_breach(request)         # late ingestion is a breach, not a fresh clock
    assign(request, rep); request.assigned_at = now
    notify(rep, payload(request))
    schedule_durably(deadline_check, request.id, at = request.due_at)

on deadline_check(request_id):
    request = reread(request_id)                            # always reread; never trust job-time state
    if request.first_touch_at and request.first_touch_at <= request.due_at: mark_met(request); return
    if now < request.due_at: reschedule(request.due_at); return
    record_breach_once(request, effective = request.due_at)
    if not request.first_touch_at:
        alert_once(rule.escalation_target, breach_payload(request), key = (request.id, "breach"))
        schedule_durably(rescue_check, request.id, at = add_covered_minutes(request.due_at, rule.sla_minutes))

on rescue_check(request_id):
    request = reread(request_id)
    if request.first_touch_at or request.rescue_attempted: return
    if backup_available(rule) and hold_time_satisfied(request):     # cooldown: one full SLA since last assignment
        reassign_once(request, backup); request.reassigned_count += 1   # original due_at is preserved
        notify(rule.duty_manager, reassigned_payload(request))
    else:
        create_manager_task(rule.duty_manager, request)     # keep named accountability; do not bounce

on qualifying_activity_logged(activity):                    # call attempt, rep-sent email, booked meeting
    request = reread(activity.request_id)
    if not request.first_touch_at or activity.occurred_at < request.first_touch_at:
        request.first_touch_at = activity.occurred_at       # trusted occurrence time, not log time
        request.outcome = "met" if request.first_touch_at <= request.due_at else "breached"
        if outcome changed: record_correction(request); supersede_alerts(request)
    cancel_pending(request)                                 # remaining jobs still reread state on wake

every N minutes (N <= shortest SLA):                        # sweeper: catches lost timer jobs
    for request in past_due_without_outcome():
        deadline_check(request.id)                          # updates the same breach record; no new alerts

HubSpot

  • Routing: a contact-based workflow triggered on Create date is known. Use if/then branches in matrix priority order; each branch ends in a Rotate contact to owner action against a team (weighted rotation is available on higher tiers) and sets matched_rule and sla_minutes.
  • Timer: a Delay of the SLA duration followed by an if/then on Last contacted is unknown. HubSpot delays don't natively pause for working hours; use the team's working-hours setting on rotation, or compute sla_due_at with a custom-code action if you're on Operations Hub.
  • Escalation: Send Slack notification action (Slack integration) or an internal email. Reassignment is another rotate action with the current owner excluded.
  • First touch: Last contacted updates on logged calls, emails and meetings; use it as first_touch_at via a workflow that copies it on first change.

Salesforce

  • Routing: Lead Assignment Rules cover the matrix in priority order for direct and queue assignment. For round robin, the classic pattern is a round-robin counter field on the lead plus assignment rule entries; for weighted or availability-aware rotation you'll want Flow with a custom object holding weights and availability, or a routing package.
  • Timer: a record-triggered Flow with a Scheduled Path at sla_due_at. Business hours are a first-class object; use BusinessHours.add() in Apex or a Flow formula to compute the due time.
  • Escalation: Chatter post, email alert, or Slack via the Salesforce-Slack integration. Reassignment updates OwnerId and increments the reassignment counter.
  • First touch: a Flow on Task/Event creation that stamps first_touch_at on the parent lead if empty.

Zapier, Make, n8n

Same shape: trigger on new lead, look up the matrix (a Google Sheet or Airtable works), update owner, then a Delay Until the due time and a conditional check. n8n handles the looping and cooldown logic more comfortably than Zapier. These are fine for under a few hundred leads a month; above that, native CRM automation or a routing tool is cheaper and more auditable.

Send it from your automation tool's HTTP action using its JSON encoder, so quotes and line breaks in values are escaped. The webhook URL contains a secret; keep it in the tool's connection settings, never in a page or a shared doc. Keep the message to request, owner, timing, action and a permission-controlled CRM link; customer notes and contact details stay in the CRM.

Plan for the alert not arriving

Save an alert event before attempting delivery, keyed by request ID and escalation stage. Retry temporary failures with bounded delays; send permanent failures to operations. Keep a CRM task as a fallback that does not depend on Slack. A network timeout can leave delivery uncertain, so an occasional duplicate message must never trigger a second reassignment; the idempotency keys in the pseudocode are what make that safe. And when alerts go quiet, check delivery and scheduler health before trusting the silence.

Section 8Metrics and review

Download the weekly metrics tracker (CSV)
Time to first touch (TTFT)
first_touch_at − received_at, for requests with a qualifying touch. Report median and p90 per queue, in covered minutes and elapsed minutes separately. The p90 is where your breaches live; the median hides them.
Time to assignment
assigned_at − lead_created_at. Should be under 60 seconds. If it isn't, your automation is batching or your enrichment step is slow.
SLA breach rate (cohort method)
Take one receipt cohort, for example last week's eligible requests. At the reporting cut-off include only those whose deadline has passed. Count a breach if the first touch was later than the deadline or is still missing. Show requests with future deadlines as pending, not as met. Worked example: 100 requests in the cohort, 90 past deadline at cut-off, of which 6 were touched late and 3 are still untouched; breach rate is 9 ÷ 90 = 10 percent. The 10 pending don't enter the denominator yet. A target under 5 percent is a reasonable operating target once stable; label it as yours, not as a universal standard. Review per queue before per rep: a bad queue number is usually staffing.
Untouched requests
Count and oldest age, per queue, at the cut-off. Percentile response times leave these out entirely, which is exactly how a queue with three abandoned enterprise leads can look healthy.
Reassignment rate
Leads with reassigned_count ≥ 1 over leads created. Above 10 percent means the matrix or the availability data is wrong. Above 25 percent and reps have learned to let leads time out.
Unmatched leads
Count landing in the catch-all row. Target zero. Every one is a missing matrix row.
Contacted and qualified rates by TTFT bucket
Bucket leads by TTFT (under 5, 5 to 15, 15 to 60, over 60 minutes) and compare contact and qualification rates. This is your own version of the MIT curve, on your own data, and it is the number that ends the argument about whether speed matters here.

Example query

select
  matched_rule                                              as queue,
  date_trunc('week', lead_created_at)                       as week,
  count(*)                                                  as leads,
  percentile_cont(0.5) within group (order by ttft_minutes) as ttft_median,
  percentile_cont(0.9) within group (order by ttft_minutes) as ttft_p90,
  avg(case when sla_breached then 1 else 0 end)             as breach_rate,
  avg(case when reassigned_count > 0 then 1 else 0 end)     as reassign_rate
from leads
where lead_created_at >= current_date - interval '8 weeks'
group by 1, 2
order by 2 desc, 1;

Weekly review, 20 minutes

  1. Oldest untouched requests first. Someone owns each one by the end of the meeting.
  2. Breach rate and p90 TTFT by queue. Any queue over target: staffing, hours, or alerting?
  3. Triage volume, split by reason. Add or fix matrix rows.
  4. Reassignments, split by cause: unavailable rep, bad match, capacity, SLA rescue. Also failed assignments, overdue timer jobs and undelivered alerts.
  5. Read three breaches and three triage cases. Separate late receipt, wrong routing, unavailable coverage, failed automation, and slow rep response. Assign each fix a person and a date.

Do not improve the breach rate by marking late requests "ineligible" after the fact. If a request was genuinely outside the agreed policy, document the evidence and correct the cohort transparently. Track meeting and opportunity outcomes separately; faster logged activity does not by itself prove a better response.

Section 9Rollout and testing

Week 1: measure

Add the fields from section 7. Backfill first_touch_at for the last 90 days from activity history. Compute current TTFT median and p90 per segment, and count requests that were never touched. Include peak days. Most teams find hours where they assumed minutes; the gap between assumed and measured is the business case, so write it down before you change anything.

Week 2: agree the policy, build the matrix, run it in shadow

Write the one-page service agreement (section 2) and get sales and operations to sign it, with a version and effective date. Tag the last 90 days of leads with the four dimensions and build the matrix from actual distribution. Then replay those historical requests through the matrix without changing owners and compare proposed assignments with what actually happened. Investigate every difference with the relevant manager. Resolve overlapping rules and unstaffed destinations before anything goes live.

Week 3: assignment and visibility

Turn on real-time assignment, deadline recording and rep notification for one queue. Turn on the T+SLA alert to the channel only. No reassignment yet. Review exceptions daily. This gives you a week of breach data with no consequences attached, which is the only way to set targets people will accept.

Week 4: controlled rescue, then expand

Run the acceptance tests below. Enable automated rescue with cooldown only after they pass. Expand queue by queue, keeping the previous rule version and a staffed manual triage path available. Hold the first weekly review. Weekly for a month, then monthly.

Minimum acceptance tests

Boundary values50, 51, 500 and 501 employees land in the intended bands; no overlapping ranges.

Write the rollback before launch

Name who can pause assignment and rescue. Pausing keeps existing deadlines, evidence and overdue reporting intact; new requests go to staffed manual triage while the previous rules are restored; work that arrived during the pause is reconciled afterwards. Keep a small change log: reason, affected rules, approver, effective time, test evidence, rollback version. Re-run the relevant tests whenever territories change, someone leaves, a channel is added, or service hours move.

The operating standard is simple to state: take any request and show who was responsible, when a response was due, what happened, and who handled the exception. If the team can answer that from the record, the system is doing its job.
